Already Done

done by the radio without asking

A phone that broadcasts hands its recordings to another node, which hands them on, and the node that finally shows them to the network waits a while before it does. Nobody watching can tell which phone they came from or when.

A listening phone publishes no address, asks nobody's directory which peers it wants, and takes a fresh network identity each time the app starts, or each day on a phone that keeps a station. Every chunk of sound is the same size, so the shape of your traffic does not say what you hear.

Everything the app keeps is sealed under a key held in the phone's own secure hardware. Station names and titles are cleaned of hidden characters, and a recorder's date and time never become a title. Copies you make leave the clipboard after a minute and a half, and the keyboard is told not to learn what you type.

Yours to Do

what no software can do for you

The radio cannot hide what you put on the air yourself.

Your voice, and the room around it
The app takes out mains hum, which can date and place a recording. It cannot take out a siren, a church bell, a train or a neighbour's voice, so record somewhere quiet. Discretion, in More, can shift your voice lower and make it not quite yours. A voice you have used anywhere else in public is a voice that can be matched.
Your words
Turns of phrase, spelling and the subjects you return to are as much yours as your face. Writing for a station in a voice you use nowhere else keeps the two apart.
Your files
Copies of music from shops, promotional services and AI music tools can carry marks in the sound itself, tied to the account that downloaded them. Re-encoding does not remove them, and nothing in the app can. Air only what you are content to have traced back to where you got it.
What your station says of itself
A home port is public, so leave it empty or name a city that is not yours. A programme that keeps clock times carries your time zone with it; one that only shuffles carries none. A Bitcoin tip address shows every payment to anyone who looks it up. Monero shows none.

Your Device

phone and computer

A phone with a screen lock is encrypted by the phone itself. Keep it locked and keep it updated, since the radio is only as safe as the phone it runs on. GrapheneOS, on a Pixel, is the hardest phone to break into that anyone can buy.

In More, under Discretion:

  1. A plain icon named Radio, and a lock screen and recents screen that say nothing.
  2. A PIN on Colours, and a second PIN to give up if you are made to open it. The second one scuttles the radio, which closes and opens next time as if newly installed.
  3. Keep only what I play, so your phone carries nothing for anyone else.

On a computer, turn on the disk's own encryption: BitLocker on Windows, FileVault on a Mac, and full-disk encryption when installing Linux. Windows and Mac then keep the radio's key in the system's own keeper as well.

Before a crossing where the phone may be searched, scuttle the radio in Colours. The 24 words, and the passphrase if you set one, bring every station back afterwards.

Your Words

the station itself

The 24 words are the station. Write them on paper, in order, and keep the paper somewhere a search of your home would not find it. Nobody from Pyrate Radio or HOSTIS will ever ask for them.

A passphrase on top of the words, set when you raise the station, makes the words alone open a different, empty station. A photograph of the paper then gives nothing away. Keep the passphrase in your head or apart from the words, because a station whose passphrase is lost is lost with it.

If a phone is lost or taken, put the words into the app on another phone. The station comes back, and the old phone can no longer speak for it.

Your Line

what your connection shows

The relay a phone or a tab connects through sees its address, and nothing of what passes. Your internet provider sees that you use the radio, and nothing of what you play or air.

For more cover, run a VPN you trust on the phone, set to always on, so the radio never touches the network without it. In a browser, open the player in Tor Browser, and the relay sees only Tor.

The app can go through Tor itself. Install Orbot, start it, and in More, under Off the grid, turn on Through Tor. From the next start the radio reaches the network only by way of Tor and offers no direct path, so no peer and no relay learns where the phone is. On a computer, Tor with its HTTP tunnel on port 8118 does the same.

Off the Grid

when the internet is cut

Any station can travel as one file. On its poster, press As a file, and pass the file by SD card, USB stick, Bluetooth or the share sheet. A phone that opens it, in More under Off the grid, plays the station at once with no network, and carries it to every radio it meets. Every entry and every chunk in the file is checked against the station's key, so a file can be carried by anyone and altered by no one.

Radios on the same Wi-Fi, or on one phone's hotspot, can find each other with no internet at all once Find radios on this Wi-Fi is on, in More, under Off the grid. Leave it off otherwise, because anyone on the same network can tell a radio is there.

Checking

that what you run is ours

Compare what you download with the checksum on the main page. The app shows the fingerprint of the key that signed it in More, under This copy, so a copy signed by anyone else can be told apart. The source is public, released as a tarball and a torrent with every version, so anyone can build it and compare.

A new version waits three days from the moment your phone first hears of it, so a release anyone was forced to sign is in front of everyone before it lands on a phone. The app says when it arrives, and you can tap to take it sooner.

The Canary

renewed every month

Once a month HOSTIS signs a line saying it has received no order to hide, change or stop anything, and no order to keep quiet about one. The app shows it in More, under The canary. If it goes more than 45 days without being renewed, the app says so, and you can read that silence for what it may mean.

The Network

the record of how it stands

Pyrate Radio has no server. Every phone with the app, every open player in a browser and every Lantern is a node, and the nodes keep the Log of stations between them. Each station is a key held by its keeper, and every entry in its log is signed by that key and checked by every node that carries it.

HOSTIS writes and publishes the software, raises its own stations, and runs one Lantern among any number anyone may run. It keeps no list of stations to refuse and ranks none. Only the holder of a station's key can remove it or change what it plays. HOSTIS holds no accounts, listening records or addresses. The stations hostis.black plays are HOSTIS's own, and every other station plays in the open player, which belongs to nobody.

Updates are signed by two of three release keys held in separate places, and carried on the network like any station. The open player lives on IPFS under a name of its own, and the app, the source and the computers' builds travel by IPFS and BitTorrent as well as from this site.
