Hard to Switch Off

how Pyrate Radio works

No server, no account and nobody in charge. Every station lives on the devices that carry it, and no single device on the way learns both who you are and what you hear.

Open the full player

every station, in any browser, nothing to install

Open the app and a station is already playing. The dial is full before the network has said a word, because your phone kept the Log from the last time it listened. The sound comes from whichever phones, computers and Lanterns hold it, and every chunk is checked against the station's own key before a note of it plays. While you listen, your own device passes the radio on for others. HOSTIS runs none of this. Take hostis.black off the internet tomorrow and every station on the dial plays on.

The Design

The Promises

There is no server

HOSTIS runs no server the radio needs. Phones, computers, browser tabs and Lanterns pass the stations between them, and a new device finds the network from any of them. HOSTIS's own machines are a few among many, and the radio runs without them.

Only the key ends a station

A station is a key made on its keeper's own device and shown once as twenty-four words. Nobody else can change what it plays or take it off the air, HOSTIS included. Every radio that plays a station keeps it and hands it on, so a station with a hundred listeners lives in a hundred places, and every radio also holds a share of the whole network, so each piece of even an unheard station sits on about eight devices. Silencing a station means reaching every one of them at once, in every country, and keeping them all offline for good.

It gets past censors

Block the ways in and the devices already aboard keep each other, and find new ones through a signed seed list and the BitTorrent DHT. Run it through Tor. Pass stations over Wi-Fi with no internet at all, or as one file on a USB stick.

Listeners stay unseen

No account, no name and no analytics. No single radio on the way learns both who you are and what you hear. A listening phone publishes no address, and its identity on the network lasts a day. Sound reaches you through two other devices. The first knows who asks and not what for, the second what is asked for and not who by, and the count beside a station is built from tokens that change every hour. While the network is young, HOSTIS's first radios may carry both hops of a new browser's route.

Broadcasters stay unseen

A broadcast leaves the keeper's device sealed, through a guard and along a stem of other radios, and surfaces about four devices from where it began. Every radio relays stems, so the first to show it cannot tell whether it made it or passed it on. Entries wait for the quarter hour, so their time stamps say nothing about when the keeper pressed the button.

Carriers hold nothing readable

A device keeps what it carries for others sealed, each chunk locked under a key worked out from the station's own log and filed under a second name, so a seized phone or a seized server shows nothing it carried. A Lantern's key opens only with its keeper's passphrase. What a listener passes on goes three hops deep, so the radio that asks never sees the device that answers.

Nothing can be forged

Every entry in a station's log is signed by its key, and every chunk of sound is checked against its fingerprint before it plays.

Not even HOSTIS can stop it

HOSTIS writes the software and raises its own stations. Its Lanterns carry every other station sealed and blind, as every radio does, so HOSTIS cannot read what they hold. It cannot take a station off the air or change what one plays, and once other radios share a route it cannot see who listens. The app's updates, the browser player's and the list a new device starts from land only when two of three release keys, kept in separate places, have signed them, and an app update waits three days on every phone, so anything HOSTIS were forced to sign is in front of everyone first. A canary signed every month says whether any order has come, and the source is CC0 for anyone to build and compare byte for byte.

No gatekeeper

Anyone can start a station in a minute, free, from a phone or a browser, and nobody approves it. Each keeper answers for what their station airs, and HOSTIS asks that it be lawful. HOSTIS ranks nothing and keeps no list of what may be played. The keeper decides what plays, and each listener decides what reaches them by hiding stations, keeping them, or taking on a list of hidden stations from someone they trust.

The Wake

where the idea came from

Pyrate Radio began with Radio Caroline. In March 1964 a ship anchored in international waters off Felixstowe and started broadcasting to a country where the BBC held the only licence to the air.

Parliament answered in August 1967 with the Marine Broadcasting Offences Act. Supplying the ships, advertising on them and working aboard all became crimes for anyone under British law. Caroline moved its base to the Netherlands and kept broadcasting. Its last ship, the Ross Revenge, ran aground in 1991 and was refloated.

In 2017 Ofcom gave Caroline a licence for the AM band, fifty years after the Act. It broadcasts to this day, and knows nothing of this radio.

Caroline needed a ship because a transmitter is a place, and a place can be boarded. Pyrate Radio plays from the phones that listen to it.

What It Would Take

to silence a voice

For a government that wants a lawful voice silenced, it would take every step below.

  1. Take down hostis.black. The full player lives on IPFS under a name of its own, pinned by anyone who runs a one-line script, and the app passes itself from phone to phone. Every station plays on.
  2. Block the ways into the network. A device already aboard remembers every radio it has met. One that knows nobody fetches a seed list signed by two of three release keys from any of several mirrors, and past that looks Lanterns up on the BitTorrent DHT, the same table millions of torrent clients use.
  3. Block the relays. Any Lantern can relay and anyone can light one, and the radio uses whichever answers quickest. Where relays are blocked, Tor carries the traffic.
  4. Spot the radio on the wire. Its traffic is encrypted QUIC that names itself HTTP/3, as a browser's does, and carries no word that says Pyrate. Tor with a bridge hides even the addresses it reaches.
  5. Seize a Lantern, or a thousand. Each chunk sits on about eight devices across the fleet and on the listeners' own radios. The rest hand it on. A seized Lantern holds everything sealed under a key that opens only with its keeper's passphrase.
  6. Seize a listener's phone. Everything the radio keeps is sealed under a key the phone's own hardware holds. An emergency PIN, given up under force, wipes it.
  7. Lean on HOSTIS. HOSTIS's Lanterns carry other people's stations sealed, and HOSTIS cannot read what they hold. Any update it were forced to make would need two of three release keys held in separate places, and would sit three days on every phone in plain view. A monthly canary says whether any order has come.
  8. Cut the internet. Radios on the same Wi-Fi find each other with no internet, and a whole station travels as one file.

The Whole Account

from the key to the speaker

The Station

a key made on your own device

Raising a station makes an Ed25519 key pair on the phone, computer or browser tab in front of you. The public half is the station's id. A call sign drawn from it is the station's readable name, which no other station can wear. The black flag that stands for it where it has no art is drawn from that id, and so is the place its frequency starts from. Frequencies run to the kilohertz, 87.500 to 108.099, which gives 20,600 places, and the keeper claims one on the station's card, the place its key points at or the next free one above. If two stations ever claim one, the one a listener keeps holds it, else the one on the air longer, and the other moves up, so no two stations share a frequency on any radio. Two stations can share a name and never a key. When two names read alike, letter for letter or with Greek or Cyrillic look-alikes, the station that has worn the name longer shows as it is and the newer says Same name beside its own.

The private half is shown to you once as twenty-four words. Whoever holds the words is the station. They stay on the device unless you write them down. Nobody keeps a copy, so nobody can recover them for you. Add a passphrase when you raise the station and the words alone open a different station, empty, so a photograph of the paper gives nothing away. The passphrase is stretched with Argon2id over 64 MiB of memory, which makes every guess at it slow and expensive.

The device that broadcasts holds a deputy key, which can put things on the air and nothing more. Only the twenty-four words can end the station, appoint a deputy or revoke one. If a phone is lost or taken, type the words into another device and appoint it, and the old phone can no longer speak for the station. A co-host holds an appointment of their own. A listener's radio shows a tip address or a cover only once it holds the station's log up to the card and the log still names that deputy.

A station's name and line, each recording, its programme, its art, every notice and live broadcast, and in the end its strike are all entries in its log. Each entry is signed by the station's key and carries the fingerprint of the one before it. Any node can check the whole log against the id alone. A carrier can hand it on, and cannot add an entry, drop one, reorder them or change a word. A crash loses at most the entry being written.

The Station's Page

what a keeper can put on it

Every station has a page, drawn from its log on the listener's own device, so it reads the same in the app and in any browser. The keeper sets a name of up to 40 characters, one line about the station of up to 140, up to five kinds (the genres it calls itself), a cover and a banner, and if they like a home port for the Local list and a Monero or Bitcoin address for tips, which listeners pay from their own wallets. Those fields ride on the station's card, and every listener's radio refuses a card past its limits unread.

Below them go notices, words to everyone who opens the page, up to 600 characters each. The newest ten show, and each can be set to come down by itself on a given day. The programme follows, with each regular show and premiere under a title of up to 80 characters and a line of up to 400, and what plays next. A keeper can also point the station at a podcast feed, and new episodes join the library as they come out.

The page shows everything as plain text. A web address typed into a notice shows as words and opens nothing when tapped, and formatting, pictures and scripts cannot ride in with the words. A link that opened on a tap would take the listener out of the radio to a site of the keeper's choosing, and that site would see the listener's address. Whoever wants the site can copy the address into their own browser.

Under the page sits the station's chat, for talk, cheers and requests the station can play next. A name in the chat runs to 32 characters and a line to 280, and each line costs the sender's device a moment of work. The radio keeps none of it after the app closes. A listener can also send the station a voice message only the station can open.

The Clock

every listener works out the same second

A station's programme is in its log. It can shuffle its library or play it in order, newest first, as a daily or weekly tape from a set hour, within on-air hours, with regular shows and one-off premieres, in the keeper's own time zone with daylight saving worked in. Each listener's device reads the programme and the time and works out what is playing, down to the chunk and the second inside it. Every listener gets the same answer, so you join mid-song as everyone aboard is hearing it. A schedule's cost falls on the station's own listeners, so the clock does its work once, passes over empty stretches and stops at a fixed amount of work. A rota naming twenty thousand recordings is worked out in a few milliseconds.

Devices keep the network's own time between them. Each compares its clock with the stations it hears, one vote to a station, and moves only when nine stations have spoken and two in three of them agree, and then by no more than ten seconds a minute, so no handful of stations can set every listener's clock wrong.

Nothing has to stay switched on for a station to broadcast. Put an album on a loop, close the app, uninstall it, and the album plays on for anyone who tunes in, because the programme and the sound live with the carriers and every listener computes the hour for themselves.

When a keeper goes live, the microphone is encoded on the device six seconds at a time, and each chunk joins the log as it is made. Listeners hear it ten to twenty seconds behind. The desk in the app and the browser mixes a microphone with a fader and a meter, a deck of files, six pads and a second input, ducks the music under the voice and limits what goes out. A keeper never hears their own station back. When the keeper ends the show, a closing entry ends it at once for every listener, and the clock takes back the hour where it has reached.

The Sound

six-second chunks, each named by what it holds

Recordings are levelled to one loudness and encoded with Opus, at 64 kbit/s for music and 24 kbit/s for talk, about 29 MB and 11 MB an hour. A station of old records can choose mono at 32 kbit/s, half the bytes of stereo for each listener. Before a voice is encoded it loses the mains hum that could date and place it, filtered out up to 1 kHz on both the 50 Hz and 60 Hz grids. Disguise my voice also lowers it by an amount drawn for each keeper from their own key. That stops a casual ear and does not beat a voice-matching program, and the switch says so. The sound is cut into chunks of six seconds, all of a station's chunks the same size, so the size of what crosses the wire says nothing about what is playing.

Each chunk is named by its BLAKE3 fingerprint, and the fingerprints are listed in the station's signed log. A device that fetches a chunk checks every byte against its fingerprint and throws away anything that does not match, so it can take sound from a stranger with no reason to trust them.

A device carrying chunks for others keeps them sealed. Each chunk is locked with a key derived from its own fingerprint and filed under a second name derived from the first. Only a device that has the station's log knows the fingerprints. Every listed station's log is public, so a carrier set on finding out what it holds can fetch the logs and match the names. On the disk every file is sealed again under a key the device keeps apart, which Unseen covers below.

The Log

every station on the air, kept by everyone

The Log is the list of stations on the air, and every node keeps its own copy. Stations announce themselves with a card (name, line, home port, kinds, cover, frequency, how many recordings it has, whether it is mature or explicit, since when it has had its name, and how far its log runs), signed by the station and passed from node to node by gossip. Each node checks each card before it keeps it or passes it on, and keeps the last Log it heard sealed on its own disk, so the dial is full the moment the app opens. A newcomer gets the six hundred most listened in one answer.

Every card carries proof of work, a puzzle that has to be solved again for each station. A station exists and plays by its link once its card carries 24 bits of work, a second or two on a phone. It reaches the dial, the list and Spin only with 28 bits, sixteen times as much, which the device climbs in the background over the minutes after raising, and only once it has something to hear, a day has passed since its first entry, and its keeper has signed a card in the last thirty days. A Log holds at most 5,000 cards, and once it is full a newcomer gets in only by showing more work than the weakest card already there. The stations you raised and the ones you keep are never at stake.

A keeper can keep a station off the dial. Then only someone holding its link hears it, and everything the network says of it, its card, who carries it and its listeners' tokens, travels sealed under a key drawn from its id and the hour and filed under a tag drawn the same way. Radios that hold the link open those notes. Every other radio passes them on unread, so nobody can list which such stations a device carries.

The count beside each station costs work too. Every listener adds a token that changes every hour, paid for in work and checked by every node, into a HyperLogLog sketch of 256 bytes. The sketch gives a number to within about six and a half percent and holds no name, so HOSTIS, the station and anyone watching can see that forty are aboard and never who the forty are.

Each listener chooses the order (most listened, newest, on the air longest, or most backed up), and HOSTIS ranks nothing. The filters are the ten kinds stations call themselves most at that moment, so nobody keeps a list of allowed genres. Each listener decides what reaches them. A hidden station leaves your Log and your dial, mature stations can be hidden and explicit ones stay off until you ask for them, pictures can be limited to the stations you keep, and lists of hidden stations pass from hand to hand for anyone who wants to use one.

Carrying

every listener holds up the network

Carry the radio while you listen is on by default. While you play a station, your device passes on what you play and other listeners' requests for any station, and a phone does it only over Wi-Fi. What you play goes out from a deck with a key of its own and no address. The deck collects the asks waiting for it from a way in along an ordinary route of three hops, your device's guard, a middle and the way in, so the one radio that may ask the deck anything sees a middle and never your device. A phone passes on no more than it listens to, a computer up to six times as much, and the deck stops when the music does. A station's own keeper and an invite-only station's members never serve it this way.

What you play also stays in your hold, sealed, until the room is needed.

Every device also holds a share of the whole Log for the fleet, listened to or not, while Help carry stations is on. The share is chosen by hashing the device's id with each chunk's name, and sized so that every chunk of every station sits on about eight devices across the fleet. While the fleet is under eight, everyone holds everything. At eighty devices each holds about a tenth, and at eight hundred about a hundredth. The hash is public, so a listener looking for a chunk works out which devices it fell to and asks them first. A phone gives up to 4 GB, or 2 percent of its free space if that is less, and fills it only on Wi-Fi while it charges. A computer gives 2 GB unless its owner chooses 8 or 20, less on a fuller disk, whenever it runs. Logs carried for others share a budget of their own, 128 MB on a phone and 1 GB on a Lantern, with no station taking more than half.

A device does the jobs the network is short of by itself. A computer, or a phone on Wi-Fi while it charges, looks every few minutes at how loaded the doors and middles it knows are, takes a job only while the network needs one, and drops it when others have it covered. A spell of either job lasts six hours at most, and then the device rests at least as long.

Press As a file on a station's poster and the whole station, log, sound and art, goes into one file to pass by SD card, USB stick, Bluetooth or the share sheet. A phone that opens it plays the station with no connection and carries it to every radio it meets. Every entry and chunk in the file is checked against the station's key.

A Spare Phone

a device given to the network

An old phone in a drawer can carry stations all day. Turn on Use this as a spare phone, leave it on a charger and Wi-Fi with the screen off, and give it 4, 16, 64 or 128 GB. A computer can do the same with its largest share and the radio left running with the window closed, or started quietly at sign-in.

A device that is on all day is the one whose address a stranger can find, so a spare phone starts only when the phone's own connection runs through a VPN or the radio goes through Tor. The app checks its own network, so a VPN set to leave the app out counts as none. If the VPN drops, the spare phone stops at once and says so, and starts again by itself when the VPN comes back. A computer has no reliable way to know, so it looks for a VPN on its route out and asks before it gives the largest share, keeps running with the window closed or lets links carry its address. Ordinary listening and carrying need no VPN and stay on for everyone.

Doors

how a browser gets aboard

A web page cannot take connections, so a browser needs something on the network that can. Listeners' apps are those doors. Let browsers in through this device is on by default on computers and on phones on Wi-Fi while they charge, and never while the radio goes through Tor. A door takes a browser's WebRTC connection and works for it as a first hop and a relay. It passes on the tab's sealed requests, hands over the cards it knows, passes on gossip and carries other radios' answers back. It cannot read what the tab asks of the network.

A listener's door publishes no address. Its note says it is there, and how busy, and nothing more. A tab already aboard asks a middle for a way in through its own route, the middle draws a door at random and passes the two their halves of the connection sealed, and only that tab learns that door's address as it connects. A door takes twelve tabs an hour at most, opens the path from its own side so no router has to forward a port, and takes a new certificate and port for every spell. It names only the address the network already sees it at, so a VPN user's door shows the VPN and never the house.

A door kept by its owner, such as a Lantern, gossips a signed note with its address and certificate, and a tab dials it straight with no signalling server between. Each build of the player carries the kept doors known when it was made. A shared link may carry two more after its station, but only from devices whose owners turned on Let shared links name this radio, and on a phone only behind a VPN or Tor. A door named by one source alone may be asked for cards and is never drawn as a guard. The same port answers STUN, so a tab learns its own address from the network's doors and asks no outside server.

A tab's route keeps its hops apart. Its exit and middle come from a relay, a network block and an introducer other than its guard's where any exists, and no one radio takes more than a quarter of its last hundred routes.

Lanterns

the radio on a machine that never sleeps

A Lantern is the radio on a Raspberry Pi, a spare laptop or a server. It starts with one Docker command and opens no ports, and it can sit behind a VPN. It relays for devices that cannot reach each other, lets browsers in as a door, holds hails for keepers until they come back, and keeps its share of the fleet. Anyone can run one. HOSTIS runs some among any number, and nothing depends on them.

A Lantern carries blind. It answers a plain request only for its own stations and those its keeper names as open. Everything else it holds and passes on sealed, as a phone does, and its web page and MP3 streams serve only its open stations. Its log names no station, peer or radio unless its keeper asks.

Its hold key is sealed under its keeper's passphrase with Argon2id and ChaCha20-Poly1305. The keeper gives the passphrase once, through a file, a keyring's own tool or the terminal. With no passphrase a Lantern opens nothing. Keys drawn from it are wiped after use. The process writes no core file when it crashes, and no other program on the machine can read or attach to its memory.

A dark Lantern runs beside Tor in one command, with no port published and every connection leaving through Tor. It holds, backs up and passes sealed data through relays, serves fewer listeners than an open one, and is never a door, since a door would name the address Tor is there to hide.

Getting Through

connections, relays and finding the network

Devices talk over QUIC, through iroh, encrypted end to end. Two devices find a direct path between them when their networks allow, punching through home routers and phone networks. When they cannot, their traffic passes through a relay, which forwards sealed bytes it cannot read. Any Lantern can be a relay. A device measures the relays it knows and homes on the quickest, and the devices keep their own address book, built from notes each carrier signs to say where it can be reached, so the network finds its own relays and leans on them before anyone else's. number0, the company that makes iroh, runs relays and an address service of its own. The radio uses neither unless its owner turns them on.

The app and Lanterns agree their keys with X25519 and ML-KEM-768 together, so traffic recorded today stays closed to a quantum computer built later. Browser tabs use X25519 alone for now.

A device starts from the radios it met before, which it keeps sealed on its disk, the radios named in a link it was handed, the network's own address book and the doors it was built with. HOSTIS's doors are one source of several. When none of them answers, it climbs further one rung at a time, and only while it is alone. First it fetches a seed list of volunteers' Lanterns that two of the three release keys have signed. Any of several mirrors serves it, and the fetch goes through Tor when the radio does. Then it looks on the mainline BitTorrent DHT, where open Lanterns leave signed notes naming themselves and their relay and no station. A phone or a computer only looks there, from a client built for the one lookup and dropped after it, so its address enters nobody's table and nothing it does writes its id anywhere. A browser that meets nobody in twenty seconds reads the same signed seed list.

With Go through Tor on (Orbot on Android, Tor's HTTP port on a computer), every connection goes by way of Tor and the radio runs dark, with no direct path and no address offered to anyone, and podcast feeds and linked pictures go through Tor too. Turn on Find radios on this Wi-Fi and radios on the same network, or one phone's hotspot, find each other with no internet at all. It is off by default, since anyone on the same Wi-Fi can hear that a radio is there.

On the Wire

what someone watching the line sees

Every connection names HTTP/3 in the clear, as a browser's does, and nothing in a handshake, a packet or a note says Pyrate. A test captures the radio broadcasting, carrying blind, listening, a tab going through a relay, a DHT lookup and a seed list being fetched, and fails if a traffic classifier names any of it as anything but ordinary QUIC, HTTP/3, TLS, HTTP, the DHT or ICMP.

The notes radios gossip say nothing about what kind of device sent them. A middle says how busy it is as a share of its own limit, so a Lantern reads like a phone, and a door never says its spell began more than six hours back. An app's identity, on a phone or a computer, lasts 24 hours from when it was made, restarts included, so no one can follow a phone from day to day by its id or pick out the phones that keep a station by seeing them come back.

The Android app looks up names at Cloudflare and Quad9 over DNS over TLS, with no plain fallback, so your provider never sees which names it asks for. Most devices test a new network with a plain web request to a captive-portal page, which anyone watching can read. The radio sends none.

What stays in view is the address a device reaches, the words a DHT lookup uses (the same for every copy of the radio), and the timing and size of the traffic. Tor with a bridge answers all three.

Unseen

listeners, keepers and the device in your hand

A listening phone tells the network nothing about what it plays, and it publishes no address. It fetches sound through two other devices. The first, a guard drawn only from radios known a week under one id and kept for weeks, sees who is asking and not what for. The second sees what is asked for and not who by. The clock tells the phone what it will need before it needs it, so the extra hops cost nothing you can hear. The first page of a station's log goes through the one guard to four exits at once, and the first answer wins. Exits are drawn from the quickest radios, and a station's own carriers come first. If a route is slow later, a second one starts beside it through another device. If the phone cannot find two devices to route through, it waits. It asks a carrier directly only if you have turned on Connect directly when needed, in Settings, under Advanced, and the station page tells you when your own station is waiting. A station looked at, and the stations either side of it on the dial brought aboard ahead of time, go the same two hops as a station played.

A broadcasting device never shows its station to the network itself, and stops answering plain requests for it. New entries and chunks leave sealed, through a guard that forwards a blob it cannot read to a second device, and that second device starts them along a stem of others, after the Dandelion++ design used in Bitcoin. At each step the cargo spreads with a chance of one in four, otherwise it moves on, so it surfaces about four devices from where it started. Every device relays stems, so the first device to show a station's new recording to the network cannot tell whether it made it or passed it on. A station kept off the dial is shown by the second device itself, which never learns who sent it. Broadcasters keep the same first step for weeks, as Tor keeps its guards, so the stem never starts with a stranger drawn at random. If no second device will take the cargo, it waits, and nothing that would let the first step tie the station to the device is handed over unless the owner has turned on Connect directly when needed. Entries that are not live are dated to the next quarter hour and leave then, so a stamp never shows when the keeper sent it, and a new station reaches others within a quarter of an hour.

On the device, everything the radio keeps is sealed under a key held in the phone's secure hardware, or by Windows' data protection, the macOS keychain or a Linux desktop's keyring on a computer. Podcast episodes and notes lie sealed too. Station names and titles are cleaned of hidden characters, a recorder's date never becomes a title, the app carries no trace of the machine it was built on, copied words leave the clipboard after ninety seconds, and the keyboard is told to learn nothing. Chatter lives in memory and is gone when the app closes. A live broadcast hides from the lock screen. Privacy and safety heads Settings. It holds a PIN on your station and settings, an emergency PIN that wipes the radio when you give it up under force, a lock screen that says only Radio, a plain icon named Radio, Go through Tor, Disguise my voice and a way to clear what you have listened to. Wiping destroys the key that seals the hold, and with it everything on the device, temporary files and hails included.

A browser tab draws a new key on every visit, reaches the network through a door or a relay, and fetches and broadcasts through two hops as the app does. When the relays are slow it may link straight to a door, or to its first hop, over WebRTC; a direct link goes around the browser's proxy and shows the device's address to the door or first hop, and Direct links in the player's settings turns it off. A tab never answers a link it did not ask for, and dials a door a shared link names only once a second source names it too. The deputy key of a station raised in a browser, any invitation, the hidden stations, the chat name and mutes and the doors it went through are sealed under a key the browser keeps to itself. That key lives in the browser's profile, so a copy of the profile can open them. A browser that cannot make that key keeps them only until the tab closes, and never in its lasting storage. Invitations and flocks go only to copies of the open player a gateway checks against their IPFS names, and the checking script itself comes from the gateway.

A copy of the open player kept in the browser, at an address that is the fingerprint of the copy itself, moves to a new version only when two of the three release keys have signed its list of files. Whoever serves an address can still replace the part of the page that does the checking, so the promise is only as good as the gateway in front of it. hostis.black's own copy keeps nothing of the kind, and serves only HOSTIS's stations; every other station is in the open player.

The parties a tab can reach are whoever serves the page (GitHub and Fastly for hostis.black; for the open player, the gateway that serves it, such as inbrowser.link on Cloudflare, dweb.link or a Lantern, and the IPFS nodes that hold its files); GitHub (raw.githubusercontent.com), which serves the signed seed list when the tab finds no one else; sslip.io's DNS, which answers for a relay's name; the relays the player knows, each of which sees the tab's address and which radios its sealed frames go to; the radios on its routes, of which the first hop sees its address; and, with Direct links on, a door or first hop it links to. With Use number0's relays when ours fail turned on in the player's settings, number0's relays and address server too, after fifteen seconds without ours. Joining a station's chatter adds the others in the room.

Joining a station's chat shows the others in it that your device listens there, and the app says so before you join. An invite-only station is hidden from everyone without an invitation and never touches the public Log, but its listeners can see each other's devices, as people in a private room can, and the app says that too.

Roads Out

the player, the app and the source

The open player lives on IPFS under a name of its own, pinned by anyone who runs a one-line script, and plays every station from any browser. The app travels by BitTorrent with a web seed, by IPFS, and from phone to phone, where any copy passes itself on with Send the app to someone nearby. The source, CC0, goes out with every version as a tarball and a torrent.

Trusting the Build

releases, keys and the canary

The Android app is signed by HOSTIS. The fingerprint of its signing certificate is printed on the main page and shown in the app in Settings, under Advanced, and every file on the site has a checksum beside it. The source is public, the toolchains are pinned and the build paths are taken out, so the same source gives the same bytes on any machine, and anyone can build the app and compare it with ours byte for byte.

Three release keys belong to three holders in separate places, each kept offline. For each release two of them sign one folder in turn, which holds the app, the open player's list of files, the list of player copies, the builds for Windows, Mac and Linux and the seed list new devices start from. Each kind of file is signed under a message of its own, so a signature over one never passes for another.

The app's updates ride the network as a station of their own. A release lands only when two of the three keys have signed it, and it waits three days from the moment your phone first hears of it, so a release anyone was forced to sign is in front of everyone before it reaches a phone. You can tap to take one sooner. The open player's fixed name moves only to a list two keys signed, and a device takes a seed list only under two signatures. The computers' builds carry their signatures beside their checksums.

Once a month HOSTIS signs a canary saying it has received no order to hide, change or stop anything, and no order to keep quiet about one. The app shows it. If it goes forty-five days without being renewed, the app says so.

The Limits

what the radio cannot do for you

Two hops keep each device on a route from learning both who asks and what for. Whoever runs both hops of a route, or watches the traffic at both ends of it, can match the two, and while the network is young that takes fewer machines. An exit, by design, learns which station it is asked for. Go through Tor goes further, and anyone whose safety rests on it should use it.

Devices that talk directly see each other's internet address, as on BitTorrent, and a relay sees the address of whoever connects through it. A door sees the address of each browser it lets in. Joining a station's chatter connects you to the others in the room. A Lantern's web page and its MP3 streams are ordinary web, and the Lantern's keeper sees who listens there. Your internet provider sees the addresses your device reaches and the timing and size of the traffic, and nothing of what you play or air. The timing of a keeper's uploads can be lined up with what the station airs a little later. Tor with a bridge, or a VPN you trust set to always on, closes that.

The radio cannot hide what a keeper puts on the air. A voice heard anywhere else can be matched, and the disguise does not stop a machine that matches voices. A room has sirens and bells in it, and music bought or downloaded can carry marks tied to the account that got it. The keeper's handbook sets out what only the keeper can do.

A phone that is unlocked and in someone else's hands is theirs. The sealing protects a locked or powered-off phone, and wiping the radio protects the one you have time to give up. A running Lantern holds its key in memory, as any running server does.

A station that no device carries any more is gone. The fleet's shares make that rare, and every Lantern and listener makes it rarer, but no design makes it impossible.

The Law

lawful use only

Pyrate Radio is for lawful broadcasting. Air your own work, work in the public domain where you and your listeners are, and work whose makers have licensed it for anyone to broadcast. Air nothing that is illegal where you are, and no one else's copyrighted work without their permission. HOSTIS condones no unlawful use of the radio and asks everyone who uses it to keep to the law.

HOSTIS raises and uploads only its own stations. Every recording on them is in the public domain or under a licence that lets anyone broadcast it, and each one is listed with its licence and source on the sources page.

Every other station belongs to whoever holds its key, and that keeper alone chooses what it plays and answers for it. HOSTIS holds no accounts, and it neither hosts, reviews, ranks, approves, runs nor profits from anyone else's station.

The privacy built into the radio protects lawful speech and every listener's ordinary right to listen unwatched, as Tor, Signal and HTTPS do. It is no licence to break the law.

HOSTIS's Lanterns carry other people's stations as every radio does, sealed and blind, and HOSTIS cannot read what they hold. Questions about a recording on HOSTIS's own stations go to hostisblack@pm.me.

The software is free under CC0 and comes as it is, with no warranty.

Questions

asked and answered
Who can see what I listen to?
No single device learns both who you are and what you hear. Your phone fetches sound through two other devices, the first knowing who asks and not what, the second what and not who. Counts are made from anonymous tokens that change every hour. What your phone carries for others sits on it sealed, and it cannot tell what it holds.
Does listening make my phone carry other people's traffic?
Yes, while you listen and on Wi-Fi only, unless you turn it off. It passes on what it plays and others' sealed requests, three hops deep, so no one it serves learns it is your device. The share it keeps for the fleet fills only on Wi-Fi while it charges.
Do I need a VPN?
Not to listen, carry or broadcast. You need one, or Tor, to make a phone a spare phone that runs all day, or to let shared links carry your device's address, because then your address is the one a stranger can find.
What may I air?
Only what the law lets you air where you and your listeners are, which means your own work, the public domain and work licensed for anyone to broadcast. HOSTIS condones no unlawful use, airs only public domain and freely licensed recordings on its own stations, and answers for no station but its own.
Can a station be taken down?
Not from any one place. Its log and its sound are copied across every device that carries it, and any one of them can hand it on. Only the holder of its key can end it.
Does my station need my phone to stay on?
No. Its programme lives in its signed log and every listener works out what is playing from the log and the clock. Put a song on a loop, uninstall the app, and the song plays on.
What happens if I lose my phone?
Put your station's twenty-four words into the app on another device and the station is yours again. The old phone can no longer speak for it. Without the words nobody can recover it, HOSTIS included.
How do people find my station?
A day after its first entry, once it has something to play, it is on the Log and on the dial at a frequency of its own. Before then, or if you keep it off the dial, pass on its link, or print its poster, which carries a code that opens the station in any phone's browser.
Can I put links on my station's page?
You can write an address in a notice, and listeners can copy it. It does not open on a tap, because a tap would send the listener to a site that sees their address. The page holds a name, a line, kinds, a cover, notices, the programme and a tip address, all as plain text.
Can I make a private station?
Keep it off the dial and only those holding its link hear it, and the network speaks of it only in notes they alone can open. Or make it invite-only. Then it never touches the Log, its key is its invitation, and only whoever you hand it to can find it. Its listeners can see each other's devices, as in a private room.
Can listeners talk?
Each station has a chat for talk, cheers and requests the station can play next, and the radio saves none of it. A listener can also send the station a voice message only the station can open.
Why “Pyrate”?
It is the spelling in Captain Charles Johnson's General History of the Pyrates of 1724, read on this radio around the clock. The old law called the pirate hostis humani generis, the enemy of all mankind. HOSTIS takes the name. The rest is at hostis.black.
